\section*{E4. Long-horizon scan (Tier 3 claimed)}

\textbf{What is scanned.} All 38 directed backbone links (19 two-way links, each direction separately, because the
photon test uses the sender at emission and the receiver at arrival), for emission times $t_e \in [0, \EfourYears]$
Julian years ($1{,}753{,}200$ h). For each link let $m(t_e)$ be the distance from the Sun to the straight segment
from the sender at $t_e$ to the receiver at its light-time arrival $t_a$. The link is \emph{closed} iff $m < 0.10$ AU.
Scheduled maintenance is one-time ($h<264$) and is handled in S1; the scan covers geometry only.

\textbf{Step size and the shortest closure it could miss.} Base step $\Delta t = \EfourStep$ h. Each point of the
segment is a fixed convex combination of its two endpoints, so if both endpoints move by at most $\varepsilon$, the
segment moves by at most $\varepsilon$ and $m$ changes by at most $\varepsilon$. Hence $|dm/dt_e| \le L$, where $L$ is
the larger endpoint speed (perihelion speed, with the factor $(1+v_{tx}/c)/(1-v_{rx}/c)$ for the stretch of $t_a$);
the largest is $L=\EfourLmax$ AU/h (Mercury). On any interval $[a,b]$ with end values $m_a, m_b$,
\[ \tfrac12\bigl(m_a+m_b-L(b-a)\bigr) \;\le\; m(t) \;\le\; \tfrac12\bigl(m_a+m_b+L(b-a)\bigr). \]
A closure that falls entirely between two grid points must therefore have grid values within
$L\Delta t/2 = \EfourMargin$ AU of the threshold, last less than 1 h, and be shallower than $\EfourMargin$ AU.
We do not leave this to the grid: every interval whose bound straddles 0.10 AU is bisected until it is certified
open, certified closed, or shorter than \EfourTolMs\ ms (\EfourEvals\ evaluations in all). The scan therefore misses
no closure or reopening longer than \EfourTolMs\ ms. \EfourSlivers\ sub-millisecond intervals stay undetermined; all
sit beside a located boundary. The shortest closure actually found lasts \EfourShortestH\ h (\EfourShortestLink),
so a step of a few hours would already have been safe; the certificate makes that a proof within the model, not a hope.

\textbf{Analytical bounds.} Relay A$\leftrightarrow$B: both relays ride one circle of radius $\sqrt8$ AU,
$90^\circ$ apart, so their chord stays $\sqrt8\cos45^\circ = 2$ AU from the Sun; motion during the 33-min flight
shifts it by under 0.01 AU. The link never closes (the scan agrees). A gateway's two relay links close only when
that relay lies almost exactly behind the Sun from the gateway; the relays are $90^\circ$ apart, so both cannot be
behind the Sun at once. The scan confirms it: the longest simultaneous closure of both relays at any settlement is
\EfourBothClosedMaxH\ h. \textbf{Every settlement can always reach every other through a route of at most three
links} (via the open relay and the never-closing A$\leftrightarrow$B link): geometric connectivity never fails in
200 years (\EfourNeverCut).

\textbf{Refined boundary (Tier 2).} The first closure on an Earth link is \EfourBndLink. The 1 h grid brackets it in
$[h\,\EfourBndLo, h\,\EfourBndHi]$ with $m = \EfourBndMlo$ and $\EfourBndMhi$ AU. After \EfourBndIter\ bisection steps
the boundary is $t_e = h\,\EfourBndT$ to within \EfourBndWidthMs\ ms (tolerance \EfourTolMs\ ms, the same as the
light-time solution). The closure lasts \EfourBndDays\ days. \emph{Effect on packets in flight:} the test applies at
launch, so the last packet emitted before the boundary flies its full \EfourBndFlight\ min and arrives; nothing
already in flight is lost to geometry. Its hop receipt goes back on \EfourBndRevLink, which closes
\EfourBndOffsetMin\ min after the boundary (at $h\,\EfourBndRevStart$), so the receipt still launches (receipt
blocked: \EfourBndReceiptBlocked). The two directions do not close together, and that creates the risky case in the
\emph{other} direction: a packet launched on \EfourBndRevLink\ during those \EfourBndOffsetMin\ min arrives at the
relay, but its receipt needs \EfourBndLink, which is already closed for \EfourBndDays\ days. The sender's hop timer
($R_h \approx \EfourBndRh$ min) expires, its retry cannot launch, and it learns of delivery only after reopening. The
relay keeps the packet ID, so a late retry is recognised as a duplicate and nothing is applied twice. Here the wait
(\EfourBndDays\ days) fits inside the 30-day packet lifetime; at Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and Ceres the longest closures run longer than
30 days, so a packet queued behind one would expire.
Our rulebook therefore forbids launching on a link whose reverse direction the known geometry closes within one
$R_h$ (paper R16), and re-routes any send that a known closure would hold more than 24 h.

\textbf{Poorest service (Tier 3).} By delay: \EfourWorstDelayPair, \EfourWorstDelay\ h one-way (window starting
at hour \EfourWorstDelayAt). By availability: a naively pinned route can lose the whole window
(\EfourWorstNaivePair, \EfourWorstNaive\% from hour \EfourWorstNaiveAt; every settlement has such windows), because
the fastest route at $h$ can close minutes later, and closures last from \EfourShortestH\ h up to \EfourLongestD\ days
(\EfourLongestLink), longer than the 30-day packet lifetime. With foresight pinning, every ordered pair of
settlements keeps \EfourWorstFore\% availability in every 24 h window of the 200 years. Routes are therefore
pinned by foresight, not by current speed (paper R15).

\textbf{Limits.} Fixed Kepler ellipses are the common model, not a forever ephemeris: these are statements about the
model. Random loss is excluded (E2 covers it); incidents are excluded (S2 covers one).
